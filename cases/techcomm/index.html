Direct recycling of battery electrodes: start with the scrap

The technology

Most lithium-ion recycling today smelts batteries or dissolves them in acid, then rebuilds cathode material from its elements. ORNL researchers patented a set of direct recycling methods that physically separate the electrode from its metal foil and reuse it with little retreatment [1][2].

One method peels the cathode film off the aluminum current collector intact using ethylene glycol, a common antifreeze ingredient, instead of shredding it into dusty fragments [2]. ORNL describes the process as cleaner, less energy-intensive and lower in impurities, and says the team has worked out how to scale it for industrial production [1].

ORNL lists these patents as available for companies to license through its Partnerships office [1].

Technology readiness

Assessed on the nine-level NASA/DOE scale. Solid: demonstrated per ORNL. Shaded: our estimate of where the scale-up work sits.

Our estimate: TRL 5–6. Validated in a relevant environment at ORNL's Battery Manufacturing Facility, the country's largest open-access battery R&D line [1], but not yet run inside a licensee's production line.

About 5% of expensive cathode material is wasted as scrap during initial production [2]. That scrap is uniform and already at the factory, so it's the obvious first market.

Commercialization scorecard

Recommended deal structure

  • Non-exclusive, field-of-use license for manufacturing scrap. Several cell makers can each use it on their own lines; no single licensee needs exclusivity to profit from its own scrap.
  • Small upfront fee, running royalty on value recovered, milestone payments at first production run and at a volume threshold. This aligns ORNL's return with real deployment instead of pricing in risk up front.
  • Keep end-of-life rights separate. Offer them later, possibly exclusive by region, to a recycler that can solve collection and mixed chemistries.
  • Pair with a cooperative R&D agreement to run the first pilot at the Battery Manufacturing Facility, moving TRL from 6 to 7 on the licensee's own material.

Who would license it

  • Cell manufacturers with new US plants: scrap from coating and slitting lines is a cost they see every day.
  • Cathode producers looking to buy back customer scrap as a cheaper feedstock.
  • Recyclers wanting a lower-cost route than hydrometallurgy for clean scrap streams.

Risks

  • Quality acceptance: recovered cathode has to requalify for cells. If it degrades performance, the value collapses.
  • Chemistry drift: manufacturers keep changing cathode formulations; a recovered material is only worth what the current recipe accepts.
  • Incumbent processes: hydrometallurgical recyclers are scaling and may price scrap collection aggressively.
  • Patent scope: needs a freedom-to-operate review against other direct-recycling patents before any exclusive grant.
